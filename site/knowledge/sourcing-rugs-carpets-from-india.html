Sourcing rugs and carpets from India

A practical map of Bhadohi, Mirzapur, Panipat and Jaipur, with construction, fibre, testing and US/EU buying considerations.

Construction changes the supplier

“Indian rug” can mean a hand-knotted carpet, hand-tufted pile, flatwoven dhurrie, kilim or a loom-made product. The technique sets lead time, price, pile behavior, design detail and repeatability. Hand-knotting suits intricate, long-life pieces; tufting can deliver a plush look at a different price and construction; flatweaves travel lighter and need their own edge and dimensional checks. Ask the manufacturer to show the actual loom and finishing process behind the quote.

The Carpet Export Promotion Council identifies Bhadohi for hand-knotted, Indo-Nepal, shaggy and tufted styles; Mirzapur for wool dhurries, kilims and loom carpets; Panipat for dhurries and tufted/shaggy products; and Jaipur for knotted wool and silk styles. Agra is another traditional carpet center. These are starting points, not exclusive specializations.

Specify what the customer will feel

Give suppliers a construction drawing, size including fringe, yarn composition, pile height, backing type, edge finish, color reference and target weight. Distinguish wool, cotton, jute, viscose, silk and synthetics: sheen and softness can be attractive, but cleaning, shedding and moisture behavior differ. Define size/shape tolerance, pile density or knot specification, color variation allowance and whether a natural-fiber variation is acceptable.

Test colorfastness to rubbing and light, dimensional stability after care, shedding, tuft bind or backing adhesion, edge durability and odor. Inspect a production lot under consistent lighting, including its reverse. Request care-label wording based on real testing; do not promise “washable” without a defined procedure and result.

Safety, labor and labeling

US indoor carpets and rugs are subject to CPSC surface-flammability standards under 16 CFR 1630 or 1631 according to size; importer certification and any special labeling depend on the product. For the EU, check fibre-composition labeling under Regulation 1007/2011 and applicable product-safety duties. Ask for evidence about subcontracted weaving and finishing, worker conditions and child-labor controls, especially if a production chain extends beyond one factory. A certificate is only one piece of due diligence; review how the order is actually made.

Commercial plan and 2026–27 view

Quote by exact size, construction and fibre, with a clear tolerance policy and a sampling charge. Ask about capacity by loom type, design-development process, dye-lot control, packing method and claims procedure. Large rolled rugs and folded flatweaves have very different freight costs and crease risks.

Our reading: buyers will favor suppliers who can prove composition, construction, safe chemistry and production visibility. That is an operational prediction, not a sales forecast.
